## model essay

The explosion of online archives, virtual exhibits, and open-access databases has transformed how we learn about culture and history. Some therefore argue that museums have outlived their purpose. I disagree. While the Internet broadens access to information, museums remain essential for preserving material heritage, providing trustworthy context, and offering irreplaceable, hands-on learning.

Admittedly, the Internet delivers clear benefits: vast scale, instant searchability, and low marginal cost. Digitised catalogues, high-resolution images, and virtual tours let learners explore collections anytime, anywhere. Yet online content also has limits. Accuracy varies, context is often thin, and algorithms reward novelty over nuance. Crucially, a screen cannot fully convey an object’s material qualities—its scale, texture, craftsmanship, and wear—that shape our understanding of the past. Nor can a webpage replicate conservation laboratories, provenance checks, or peer-reviewed curation that help the public distinguish evidence from myth.

Museums perform roles the Internet cannot. They safeguard artefacts under controlled conditions, conserve fragile objects, and document provenance to protect against looting and forgery. They also curate narratives that connect objects to place, time, and people, turning fragments into coherent stories. Educational programmes, community exhibitions, and tactile experiences engage visitors of all ages and strengthen a shared sense of identity and belonging. Far from being replaced by the web, leading museums now complement their galleries with digital access, making collections both physically and virtually available.

In short, online information is a powerful complement, not a substitute. To preserve cultural memory and promote informed citizenship, societies should invest in both robust digital platforms and vibrant, well-resourced museums.
