## model essay


Leaving high school, many young people lack the skills to manage money effectively. This happens largely because schools seldom teach financial literacy, and because the transition from a small allowance to a full salary is sudden. Fortunately, targeted education and guided practice can close this gap.

One reason for weak money management is curricular: most secondary schools prioritise examinable subjects and treat budgeting, saving, interest, credit, and taxes as optional extras. As a result, graduates can recite historical dates or chemical formulas yet struggle to read a pay slip, compare loans, or plan a monthly budget. A second reason is psychological. While in school, students typically live on fixed allowances that cover essentials with little left for discretionary spending. When they begin earning, the abrupt jump in income—combined with aggressive marketing and easy digital payments—can trigger impulsive purchases and lead to debt.

To address these causes, financial literacy should be a compulsory component of the high-school curriculum. Practical modules—creating a zero-based budget, setting up an emergency fund, understanding compound interest, taxes, insurance, and responsible use of credit—should be assessed through real-life tasks rather than rote tests. In parallel, schools can run simulations: give students a mock monthly income, unexpected “bills,” and saving goals so they learn to prioritise under pressure. Parents also play a crucial role. Gradually shifting from weekly allowances to larger, less frequent budgets (for a term or semester) teaches planning, while matching contributions to a child’s savings builds habits. Clear guardrails—such as spending logs and end-of-month reviews—turn guidance into measurable progress.

In conclusion, students’ poor money management stems from limited instruction and a sudden change in financial circumstances. Mandatory, hands-on education at school, reinforced by structured practice at home, can equip young people to make informed, disciplined financial decisions.
