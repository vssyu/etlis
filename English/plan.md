## Model Essay


Some argue that planning for the future is a waste of time and that we should focus only on the present. I disagree. While living in the moment reduces stress and helps people act with clarity, planning—done flexibly—provides direction, safeguards against risk, and turns aspirations into achievable steps.

Planning matters because it aligns daily effort with long-term goals. With a clear roadmap, people can track progress, prioritise tasks, and allocate limited resources such as time and money. Organisations do this through strategic plans; individuals do it with savings targets, study schedules, or career milestones. Crucially, good plans are adaptable: they anticipate uncertainty, build in buffers, and are reviewed regularly. Without any plan, people are more likely to drift, miss deadlines, and make reactive decisions that prove costly.

That said, an exclusive future focus can backfire. The future is inherently uncertain, and over-planning every detail often produces anxiety and decision paralysis. Concentrating on the present—one task at a time—improves attention, enjoyment, and performance. Students who break large goals into daily study blocks, and workers who practise single-tasking rather than constant forecasting, tend to sustain momentum and avoid burnout. Present-focused habits are the engine that move a flexible plan forward.

The most effective approach blends both horizons: set simple, measurable long-term objectives, then execute through short, present-focused routines. Review the plan periodically, adjust when circumstances change, and return attention to the next actionable step. In this way, planning guides direction while present focus powers execution.

In conclusion, it is not a waste of time to plan for the future; it is a waste to plan rigidly and then ignore the present. A flexible plan, implemented through mindful day-to-day actions, offers the best of both worlds.
