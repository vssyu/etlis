## model essay

Some people argue that it is acceptable to use animals for human benefit, while others believe exploiting them is morally wrong. Both perspectives have valid arguments, but I personally think animals can be used for human purposes under strict regulation.

Supporters contend that animal use has saved and improved countless human lives. In medicine, for example, pre-clinical studies on animals can detect toxicity and efficacy before human trials, preventing severe harm. Vaccines, anaesthetics, and many life-saving therapies were refined through carefully controlled animal studies. Beyond healthcare, assistance animals and humane livestock systems can contribute to food security and disability support when managed responsibly. From this perspective, conditional use is ethically permissible if human benefits are substantial and no viable non-animal method exists.

Opponents counter that animals are sentient and should not be instrumentalised. They point to suffering in poorly regulated laboratories and intensive farming, and to ecological harms when species are overharvested. They also argue that alternative methods—organoids, computer models, and advanced imaging—can often replace animal procedures, undermining the necessity claim. For them, the moral cost outweighs the gain.

My view reconciles these positions. Society should authorise animal use only as a last resort, with strict oversight: independent ethics review, transparent reporting, and severe penalties for violations. Simultaneously, we should accelerate funding for non-animal methods so dependence continually declines. In this way, human interests are protected while needless animal suffering is minimised.

In conclusion, blanket acceptance or rejection is unhelpful. Limited, well-regulated use of animals can be justified in high-stakes contexts, but the ethical bar must remain high and the trajectory should be toward ever fewer animal procedures.
