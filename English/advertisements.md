## model essay


Advertising has long divided opinion. Supporters argue that it boosts the economy by informing consumers and stimulating competition. Critics counter that it fuels dissatisfaction by promoting unrealistic lifestyles and constant consumption. In my view, advertising delivers clear economic benefits, but those gains are strongest when rules curb deceptive or harmful messages.

On the positive side, advertising reduces “search costs”: it tells people what products exist, what they cost, and where to find them. This helps new and small firms reach customers, increases competition, and can lower prices. Ads also fund free or low-cost services—news, sports broadcasts, and many apps—so consumers enjoy more content without paying directly. When consumers are better informed and businesses can signal quality or innovation, markets tend to grow and jobs are created.

Critics respond that advertising can damage well-being by cultivating dissatisfaction and status anxiety. Commercial messages frequently idealise lifestyles and bodies, implying that happiness depends on buying the next product. This can drive impulsive spending, consumer debt, and harmful comparisons—especially among teenagers. Highly personalised digital ads also blur the line between persuasion and manipulation, exploiting data to trigger fear of missing out. When people measure self-worth against curated images and luxury brands, frustration with “who I am” and “what I have” can grow.

On balance, advertising’s economic role is positive, but the social risks are real and manageable. Truth-in-advertising rules, clear labelling of sponsored content, limits on harmful ads to children, and stronger data-privacy standards can restrain excesses. At the same time, teaching media and financial literacy helps people interpret ads critically. With sensible safeguards, societies can keep the economic upside while reducing avoidable harm.

In conclusion, advertising does risk fostering dissatisfaction, but, with fair rules and informed consumers, its economic advantages outweigh its social costs. The goal is not more advertising but better advertising—truthful, transparent, and age-appropriate.
