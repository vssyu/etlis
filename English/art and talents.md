## Model Essay

Art sits on a spectrum between everyday expression and exceptional mastery. Some argue that only people with rare talent can create true art, while others believe everyone has the capacity to make it. I agree with the latter view: artistic expression is a universal human ability, though talent and training often determine how far an artist can take that expression.

Those who claim that everyone can create art point to how naturally people make and respond to symbols. Children draw what they see, hum what they hear, and invent stories long before they receive formal instruction. Adults do the same in more subtle ways—snapping photos, decorating spaces, or keeping journals. These acts may lack virtuoso technique, but they still translate feelings and ideas into form. In this sense, art is not a gated profession but a basic human language available to all.

Opponents counter that art worthy of public recognition demands exceptional ability. Master composers, painters, and dancers combine sensitivity, vision, and technique that most people cannot match. They argue that without innate aptitude and rigorous training, people may produce pleasant crafts but rarely works of lasting impact. This view captures an important truth: excellence is scarce, and the bar for professional artistry is high.

My view reconciles these positions. Everyone can create art because everyone can shape meaning through sound, image, movement, or words. At the same time, talent and disciplined practice raise the ceiling, enabling a minority to produce groundbreaking work. A healthy society should encourage broad participation—through arts education and inclusive platforms—while also supporting advanced training for those who pursue mastery.

In conclusion, art is both universal and stratified: open to all as a mode of expression, yet elevated by talent and training. Recognizing both realities invites more people to make art, and helps the most gifted develop their full potential.
