## model essay


Some argue that governments should focus exclusively on their own citizens because it is impossible to help everyone worldwide. I disagree. While elected leaders have a primary duty at home, targeted international assistance is both morally justified and pragmatically wise in an interdependent world.

There are sufficient reasons to prioritise domestic needs: governments are accountable to their taxpayers, public budgets are finite, and core obligations—such as healthcare, education, and social protection—must be funded reliably. However, this does not imply turning inward. Problems that cross borders—pandemics, climate change, conflict, and forced migration—cannot be contained by national policy alone. Strategic aid abroad can prevent crises from escalating and ultimately protect lives and livelihoods at home.

International assistance also delivers high returns when it is well designed. Modest investments in vaccination campaigns, clean water, disaster preparedness, or green energy in lower-income countries can avert costly emergencies, stabilise regions, and create future trading partners. Moreover, cooperation enhances a country’s reputation and influence, strengthening diplomatic ties and opening doors for cultural and scientific exchange.

The most sensible path is a balanced one. Governments should meet domestic responsibilities first, but commit a predictable share of resources to effective, transparent programmes overseas—ideally in partnership with reputable multilateral organisations and local institutions. Support should emphasise capacity building, anti-corruption measures, and a just transition for workers, so benefits endure.

In conclusion, it is unrealistic to help everyone, but it is short-sighted to help no one beyond one’s borders. A dual commitment—robust services at home and smart, accountable assistance abroad—best serves citizens and the wider world.
