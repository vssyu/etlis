## model essay

Many developing countries are expanding tourism because it promises quick job creation, foreign-exchange earnings, and infrastructure investment with relatively low entry costs. Overall, I believe this is a positive development—if governments manage growth carefully and avoid overdependence.

First, tourism monetises assets many countries already possess: beaches, wildlife, heritage sites, cuisine, and festivals. Marketing these draws in external demand, so local firms—from guesthouses to transport and crafts—gain customers without relying solely on domestic purchasing power. Second, visitor spending broadens the tax base, enabling investment in roads, water, sanitation and airports that also benefit residents. Third, digital platforms and cheaper air travel have lowered the cost of attracting international visitors, making tourism a realistic near-term engine of growth while longer-gestation industries scale up.

However, unplanned expansion can cause problems: copy-and-paste resorts lead to price wars and low service quality; seasonal demand creates unstable incomes; and rapid development can strain ecosystems and communities. The answer is not to reject tourism but to shape it—differentiate offerings, cap numbers at fragile sites, strengthen local supply chains so more spending stays in-country, and invest part of the revenue in skills and infrastructure beyond tourism. In this way, tourism becomes a bridge to broader development rather than a trap.

In conclusion, the turn toward tourism is understandable and, with smart policy, beneficial. Managed for quality and sustainability—and paired with economic diversification—it can raise incomes today while laying foundations for tomorrow.
