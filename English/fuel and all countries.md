## model essay 


The world must cut its reliance on oil and gas quickly, but the way we get there matters. I agree that the international community should act now to drive down fossil-fuel use. However, expecting every nation to reduce consumption at the same speed is unrealistic and unfair. Urgency should be paired with support and differentiated responsibilities.

Acting immediately is justified on several grounds. First, fossil-fuel combustion drives air pollution and climate risks, harming health, agriculture, and infrastructure. Second, delaying the transition locks in expensive assets and exposes economies to volatile fuel prices. Clear global signals—such as standards for efficiency, phased targets for power and transport, and large-scale investment in clean energy and grid upgrades—can accelerate innovation and reduce costs for everyone.

That said, countries start from different points. Many developing economies rely on energy-intensive industries for jobs and exports, have limited grids, and face tight public budgets. For them, cutting consumption overnight could trigger unemployment and social instability. A credible plan should therefore combine near-term actions (ending wasteful subsidies, improving efficiency, and tackling methane leaks) with support that enables deeper cuts over time. This includes affordable finance for clean power and transit, technology transfer, and training for workers in a just transition. Meanwhile, high-income countries—whose historical emissions and fiscal capacity are larger—should move fastest and fund part of the transition elsewhere.

In conclusion, I strongly agree that the world must move immediately to reduce fossil-fuel consumption, but timelines and burdens should reflect national circumstances. With firm global rules, ambitious leadership from advanced economies, and practical support for others, rapid and fair reductions are achievable.
