## model essay
Across many economies, people are switching careers more often than in the past. The main reasons are rapid technological change, shifting personal values, and more flexible labour markets. Overall, I consider this trend positive for society, provided that governments and employers support reskilling and protect people during transitions.

A first driver is technology. Automation and new digital tools regularly reshape job content and create fresh roles that did not exist a few years earlier. Workers move to keep their skills relevant and to access better pay and advancement. A second reason is changing priorities. Many people seek work that aligns with their interests, health, and family life, and are more willing to pivot when a role offers limited progression or poor work–life balance. Finally, switching costs have fallen. Online courses, professional certificates, and remote work make it easier to retrain and to enter adjacent industries without relocating.

For society, frequent career change brings clear benefits. It improves the match between skills and demand, spreads ideas across sectors, and often boosts productivity when people carry methods from one field into another. Dynamic labour flows also encourage entrepreneurship and lifelong learning. However, there are risks. Frequent moves can mean income volatility, loss of seniority and benefits, and uneven opportunities for those with limited resources or for older workers who face hiring bias. Without safety nets and affordable training, the trend may widen inequality.

On balance, career mobility is socially beneficial if managed well. Policies that recognise prior learning, fund mid-career training, and provide portable benefits can turn individual pivots into collective gains.
